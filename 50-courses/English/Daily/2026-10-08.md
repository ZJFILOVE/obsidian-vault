## Why this specific material is worth using

The article develops a causal narrative: **origin → movement → accumulation → consequences → responses**. It teaches you to move from physical mechanisms (currents and wind) to environmental, economic and safety consequences, then to compare different responses. This is useful for reading nonfiction and for explaining unfamiliar topics without exaggerating the evidence.

Rather than memorizing marine vocabulary, focus on transferable verbs: **be dispersed**, **trace something back to**, **pose a risk**, **prevent something from happening**. Today's skill is identifying whether a sentence describes a **mechanism**, **possible harm**, **observed example**, or **recommended action**.

## Essential caveats — what the title and images do not prove

A so-called **garbage patch** is not necessarily an island-shaped, solid floating mass. NOAA explains that debris may be widely dispersed and that some of it is hard to see; wind, currents, and seasonal conditions change where it accumulates. Marine debris includes more than plastic. Finding an object far from land does not necessarily reveal its origin. The page is an educational overview, not a newly published survey establishing the total amount of marine debris in 2026.

**Prevention** and **cleanup** are complementary: removing existing material does not by itself prevent new material from entering a waterway.

## Before reading — predict the categories (3 min)

Without consulting the article, give a one-line definition of each:

1. **Source:** where an item first entered the water.
2. **Transport mechanism:** what moved it.
3. **Exposure:** where a person, vessel, or animal encountered it.
4. **Consequence:** what changed because of that encounter.
5. **Response:** what might prevent or reduce the problem.

Test the ambiguity: can one know where an object was **found** without knowing where it **came from**?

## Grammar and collocation clinic

### Even though ... , ...

A concession introduces something that is true without removing the main point.

Original example: **Even though two parcels leave the same warehouse, their delivery times may differ.**

### Can result in ... versus always causes ...

**Result in** links cause and outcome. **Can result in** expresses possibility, not certainty.

Original example: **A missing verification step can result in errors that are difficult to correct later.**

### Be difficult to trace back to ...

Used when provenance is uncertain.

Original example: **After a rumor has been reposted repeatedly, its original source may be difficult to trace.**

### Prevent A from doing B

Form: **prevent + object + from + -ing**.

Original example: **Clear instructions can prevent visitors from entering the wrong area.**

### Rather than ...

Use an explicit alternative, rather than only saying something is good or bad.

Original example: **Rather than tracking clicks alone, measure whether users finish the task.**

## Precision rewrite — five unsupported claims

Rewrite these so that each is compatible with the source. Do not reproduce the article's wording.

1. Garbage patches are permanent solid islands.
2. The origin of any object found in the ocean can be identified.
3. Ocean debris damages animals but has no economic impact.
4. Annual beach cleanup completely eliminates future litter.
5. One technique works for every type and location of debris.

> [!answer]- Suggested rewrites
> 1. Accumulation zones contain debris that may be widely dispersed and whose distribution can shift.
> 2. Transport over long distances often makes the origin of debris difficult to identify.
> 3. Marine debris can threaten wildlife, habitats, tourism, navigation, and safety.
> 4. Beach cleanup can remove some existing debris, but preventing new waste requires additional action.
> 5. The appropriate response depends on local conditions and the kind of debris involved.

## Speaking output — 90 seconds

A friend says, “There is a giant island of trash in the ocean. If ships collect it, the whole problem will be solved.”

Reply in four stages:

1. Acknowledge the benefit of removing existing debris.
2. Correct the picture of a continuous solid island.
3. Explain how wind and currents spread or accumulate material.
4. Distinguish removal from preventing additional waste.

Use **at least four** today's chunks: **be dispersed**, **trace back to**, **pose a risk to**, **prevent ... from**, **address the root cause**.

## Three comprehension / output questions

1. **Comprehension:** Name three forces or environmental features that affect the movement of marine debris. Why can that movement make source attribution difficult?
