## Why this is specifically worth using

“Opportunity cost” is often taught as a one-line definition: **the value of the next-best alternative you give up when you make a choice**.

The useful part of this article is that it forces you to apply that definition to very different decisions: leisure versus study, one crop versus another, driving versus taking a train, upgrading a car, spending a windfall, and making small repeated purchases.

That makes it good English practice for a reasoning pattern that appears far beyond economics:

**choice → alternative → sacrifice → comparison → uncertainty → decision**

The language is reusable in essays, presentations, lab planning, budgeting, project management, and everyday discussion. You will repeatedly need to distinguish:

- a visible price from a less visible cost;
- a direct payment from time or flexibility lost;
- a current benefit from a future alternative;
- a numerical illustration from a guaranteed outcome.

Today's goal is not to become more frugal. It is to become more precise when you describe what a decision actually costs.

## Important caveat — opportunity cost is a framework, not a forecast

The article includes examples in which money is hypothetically invested at a fixed annual return. Those examples are **illustrative**, not predictions.

The page explicitly notes that its compound-interest examples do not account for inflation and taxes, and it also states that opportunity cost cannot be predicted absolutely. Real alternatives have uncertain returns, different risks, and nonfinancial value.

So do not convert:

> “If I spend X now, I definitely lose Y in the future.”

into a universal rule.

A better formulation is:

> **Spending X now means giving up the chance to use that money, time, or attention for another purpose; the value of that forgone alternative may be estimated, but it is often uncertain.**

This distinction matters in academic English: **counterfactual reasoning is not the same as observing an outcome that actually occurred.**

### First-pass rule

Do not treat the article as saying that every purchase should be avoided.

A choice can have a real opportunity cost and still be worth making.

The question is not:

**“Does this choice cost me something?”**

Almost every meaningful choice does.

The better question is:

**“Is the benefit I receive worth more to me than the best alternative I am giving up?”**

## Precision drill — opportunity cost is not the same as sunk cost

These two ideas are often confused.

### Opportunity cost

Forward-looking. It asks:

> **What valuable alternative am I giving up by choosing this option now?**

### Sunk cost

Backward-looking. It refers to resources that have already been spent and cannot be recovered.

Example:

You bought a nonrefundable ticket yesterday. Today you are sick.

- The ticket price is already a **sunk cost**.
- The opportunity cost of going today may include rest, recovery time, and whatever else you could do instead.

Good decision-making should not automatically favor an option merely because you have already spent money on it.

## Output task — defend a choice without pretending it has no cost

Give a **90-second spoken answer**:

**Describe one choice that has a real opportunity cost but may still be worth making.**

Possible domains:

- taking a slower but less stressful commute;
- paying more for a durable product;
- taking a day off;
- joining a course;
- traveling;
- saving instead of spending;
- spending instead of saving.

Structure:

1. State the choice.
2. Name the next-best alternative.
3. Identify one explicit cost.
4. Identify one implicit cost.
5. Name one benefit that could justify the choice.
6. State one uncertainty.
7. Conclude without claiming there is one universally correct answer.

Try to use at least five items from today's vocabulary:

**trade-off, forgo, allocate, weigh A against B, implicit cost, sunk cost, marginal, lock in, be better off**

## Three comprehension / output questions

1. **Concept:** Why is opportunity cost defined by the next-best alternative rather than by every option that was rejected?
2. **Application:** How can a decision with a higher money price still have a lower total opportunity cost for a particular person?
3. **Evaluation:** Why should the article's compound-interest examples be treated as illustrations rather than forecasts of what a specific person will earn?

For Question 3, use:

> **The example assumes ___. In reality, ___. Therefore, it illustrates ___ rather than proving ___.**
