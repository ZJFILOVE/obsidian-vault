## Why this is specifically worth using

Today's article trains a kind of English that textbooks often undertrain: deciding **how much to say, to whom, in what setting, and for what purpose**.

The useful reasoning pattern is:

**possible disclosure → perceived risk → possible relational benefit → context check → response from the other person → adjustment**

A sentence can be grammatically perfect and still be socially poorly calibrated. Conversely, a small amount of appropriate openness can make speech feel less scripted and can help people build trust.

This is valuable for seminars, lab meetings, interviews, group work, and ordinary English conversation.

## Important caveat — interview evidence is mixed

The piece is an edited conversation with behavioral scientist Leslie John about ideas developed in her book **Revealing**. It mixes several evidence types:

- behavioral and organizational research;
- the interviewee's interpretation of that research;
- personal anecdotes;
- practical advice;
- broader claims about relationships.

Keep those levels separate.

An anecdote can illustrate a mechanism without proving a universal rule. A research finding can describe an average tendency without telling you exactly what to say in every workplace or relationship.

A careful takeaway is:

**People may overestimate some risks of appropriate openness, but what counts as appropriate depends on context, timing, reciprocity, purpose, and power.**

## Precision drill — four related ideas

### disclosure

The broad act of sharing information about yourself.

### transparency

Making your reasoning, constraints, preferences, or decision process visible.

### vulnerability

Sharing something that creates meaningful social or emotional risk.

### oversharing

Sharing more than the relationship, setting, timing, or purpose can comfortably support.

The key point is that these words do not describe the same thing.

## Language-focus drill — soften without becoming vague

Rewrite the blunt versions into natural, clear English.

1. You made the discussion awkward.
2. I don't want to answer that.
3. You didn't give me credit.
4. I need time before I answer.
5. That question is too personal for this setting.

Useful frames:

- **I wasn't quite sure how to respond to...**
- **I'd rather leave that aside for now.**
- **I wanted to clarify how the contribution was presented.**
- **Could I take a moment to organize my thoughts?**
- **I'm not very comfortable getting into that in this setting.**

## Output task — calibrated communication

Speak for **90 seconds** on this prompt:

**A student needs to tell a supervisor that they are overloaded and falling behind. How can they be honest while keeping the conversation relevant and professional?**

Use this structure:

1. identify the practical goal;
2. separate facts from feelings;
3. include only relevant context;
4. acknowledge the power relationship;
5. make one concrete request;
6. adjust based on the response;
7. end with a non-absolute principle.

Try to use at least five items from today's vocabulary:

**open up, hold back, build rapport, come across as, reciprocate, read the room, put someone on the spot, confide in, draw a boundary**

## Three comprehension / output questions

1. Why can withholding information carry costs even when it reduces immediate embarrassment?
2. Why is reciprocity more useful than a rule such as “always be more open”?
3. What makes openness at work different from openness with a close friend?

**power imbalance + professional purpose + future consequences + context**
