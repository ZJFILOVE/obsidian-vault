## Why this is worth using

This is a compact introduction to the International Phonetic Alphabet and the physical constraints behind human speech sounds. It is useful English practice because the narration is fast but very clear, and the topic forces you to distinguish between closely related ideas such as **possible**, **attested**, **articulated**, and **meaningful in a language**.

One caveat is worth keeping in mind: the title is more dramatic than the actual scope of the video. Treat it as a short IPA/articulation lesson rather than as a complete survey of every theoretically possible human speech sound. That makes it a good exercise in both listening and evaluating what a source actually claims.

## Study routine

### First pass — gist, no captions
Watch straight through once. Do not pause for unfamiliar terms. Then answer in one or two English sentences:

**Why can the human vocal tract be physically capable of producing a distinction that a particular language does not use?**

### Second pass — structure and language
Watch again with captions and note 5 useful chunks. Pay particular attention to how the speaker describes:

- parts of the mouth and vocal tract;
- physical/articulatory constraints;
- the difference between a possible sound and a sound used contrastively in a language;
- examples used to explain the IPA chart.

### Output — 60 to 90 seconds
Without the video, explain the difference between:

1. a sound a human can physically produce;
2. a sound that occurs in a known language;
3. a sound difference that changes meaning in a particular language.

Do not worry about perfect terminology on the first attempt. The goal is to make the distinctions clear.

### Optional shadowing
Choose about 30–45 seconds where Tom explains the IPA chart. Shadow it twice: first for pronunciation, then for rhythm and stress.

## Comprehension and output

1. What information does the IPA try to represent, and why is a chart useful for doing this?
2. Why are some positions or combinations in a speech-sound chart physically difficult or impossible?
3. Why is “humans can make this sound” not the same claim as “this is a phoneme in a language”?
