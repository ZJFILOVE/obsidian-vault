## Why this is specifically worth using

This is a particularly good English-learning source because it does not present scientific discovery as a single dramatic moment. Instead, it shows a **verification pipeline**:

**notice a possible signal → collect more data → rule out alternatives → seek independent support → submit the result to scrutiny → publish and catalog the finding**.

That structure appears far beyond astronomy. The same language is useful when reading papers in chemistry, medicine, engineering, psychology, and data science. A strong academic reader needs to notice the difference between:

- **seeing a pattern** and **knowing what caused it**;
- **a candidate explanation** and **a confirmed explanation**;
- **one measurement** and **independent evidence**;
- **an observation that is consistent with a hypothesis** and **evidence strong enough to rule out major alternatives**.

The article is also useful because the topic is unfamiliar enough to force careful reading without being so specialized that the vocabulary becomes overwhelming.

## Important caveat — the method is useful, but some mission-status details are historical

The page was published in **2018**. Its methodological discussion is still useful for learning how scientists move from a possible exoplanet signal toward stronger evidence, but several references to telescope status are clearly tied to that publication date.

For example, the article speaks about missions such as TESS, Kepler, Spitzer, and the James Webb Space Telescope from the perspective of 2018. Treat those mission-status statements as **historical context**, not as a current 2026 mission update.

This is an important reading habit: a source can remain valuable for its **conceptual explanation** while some time-sensitive details become outdated.

Today, therefore, separate:

1. **methodological claims** — how detection and verification work;
2. **historical mission context** — what NASA expected or reported in 2018;
3. **general scientific-language patterns** — how the text expresses uncertainty and confirmation.

## First pass — reconstruct the verification chain

Read the article once from beginning to end without stopping for every technical term.

Your goal is to recover the following six-step chain:

1. **Initial detection:** what kind of observation can suggest that an exoplanet may be present?
2. **Pattern recognition:** why does a repeated dip or other recurring signal matter?
3. **Alternative explanations:** what non-planet causes must scientists consider?
4. **Independent support:** why is a second telescope or detection method useful?
5. **Scientific review:** what happens before a result becomes part of the formal literature?
6. **Classification:** why are words such as *candidate*, *validated*, and *confirmed* not interchangeable?

After the first pass, close the page and write a six-line map from memory. Each line should contain one verb:

**detect → compare → exclude → corroborate → review → classify**

### First-pass rule

Allow yourself at most **five lookups**. Do not look up every astronomy noun. If you understand the role of a term in the argument, keep moving.

For example, you do not need a full astrophysics lesson on brown dwarfs to understand why the article mentions them. You only need to recognize that they are one possible **alternative explanation** for a signal.

## Second pass — mark claim strength

Read again and label key sentences with one of these categories:

- **O = observation** — what the telescope recorded;
- **H = hypothesis / candidate interpretation** — what the observation may indicate;
- **A = alternative explanation** — what else could produce the same pattern;
- **T = test** — what additional observation or method is used;
- **E = evidence strengthening the claim** — what makes the planet interpretation more credible;
- **Q = qualification** — wording that limits certainty;
- **P = publication / peer review** — how the result enters the scientific record.

The central logic is not:

**we saw a dip → therefore there is a planet**.

It is closer to:

**we saw a repeatable pattern → the pattern is consistent with a planet → other causes must be tested → independent observations can strengthen the interpretation → the claim is reviewed and classified according to the strength of the evidence**.

That is a reusable template for scientific reasoning.

## Language targets for the second pass

Collect short reusable chunks rather than copying source sentences:

- **look for a recurring pattern**
- **indicate the presence of...**
- **be consistent with...**
- **rule out alternative explanations**
- **follow up with another method**
- **independent observations**
- **provide stronger evidence for...**
- **be more than 99 percent certain**
- **submit a study for peer review**
- **distinguish a candidate from a confirmed result**

Use these in your own examples from any field.

## Precision task — observation is not interpretation

For each case below, write two sentences:

1. a sentence that reports only the **observation**;
2. a second sentence that gives a **cautious interpretation**.

### Case A
A star becomes slightly dimmer at regular intervals.

### Case B
A sensor in a laboratory produces an unexpected peak in repeated measurements.

### Case C
Students who used a new study app obtained higher average scores than students who did not use it.

Your second sentence should use one of these:

- **may indicate**
- **is consistent with**
- **could reflect**
- **does not by itself establish**
- **requires further testing**

The purpose is to stop yourself from turning a pattern directly into a causal conclusion.

## Mini reasoning task — what would count as stronger evidence?

Imagine that a telescope records three regular decreases in brightness from the same star.

Write three possible next steps in English:

1. one that checks whether the pattern repeats;
2. one that looks for an alternative explanation;
3. one that gathers evidence using a different method or instrument.

Then rank the three steps from **weakest** to **strongest** evidence and explain your ranking in 3–4 sentences.

The exact ranking is less important than your reasoning. Your explanation should use at least two of:

**independent, alternative, corroborate, artifact, false positive, robust**.

## Read-aloud / shadowing task — 3 × 45 seconds

Choose one paragraph from the sections on ruling out non-planet explanations, using a second detection method, or peer review.

### Round 1 — thought groups

Mark `/` between information groups. Stress content words such as:

**signal, candidate, evidence, method, telescope, alternative, confirm**.

### Round 2 — uncertainty words

Read again and deliberately preserve the strength of words such as:

**may, could, possible, strong sign, more than, confirm, validate**.

Do not flatten them into the same level of certainty.

### Round 3 — reformulate

Close the page and explain the paragraph in your own words for 30–45 seconds.

Do not try to reproduce the article. Preserve only the logical relation:

**what was observed → why it was not enough → what additional evidence was needed**.

## Output task — when should we believe a surprising result?

Give a **90-second spoken response** to this question:

**What should happen before a surprising scientific result is treated as reliable?**

Use this structure:

1. State why an interesting signal is not automatically a discovery.
2. Give one example of an alternative explanation.
3. Explain why independent evidence matters.
4. Mention replication, peer review, or another form of scrutiny.
5. End by explaining why scientific caution is not the same as indecision.

Try to use at least five of today’s vocabulary items:

**candidate, detect, false positive, rule out, corroborate, artifact, tentative, robust, converge**.

## Comprehension and output questions

1. **Evidence:** Why can a recurring dip in a star’s brightness be treated as a promising signal without being treated as final proof of a planet?
2. **Method:** Why does evidence from a second telescope or a different detection method strengthen a claim more than simply repeating the same interpretation of the original data?
3. **Evaluation:** The article distinguishes several stages between a possible signal and a published discovery. Which stage do you think is most important for preventing false conclusions, and why?

## Claim-strength challenge

Rewrite each sentence so that it becomes more defensible.

1. The telescope found a planet because the star became dimmer.
2. Repeating the measurement proves that the interpretation is correct.
3. Peer review guarantees that a published result is true.
4. Two methods gave similar results, so no alternative explanation is possible.

Useful tools:

- **provides stronger evidence for**

- **does not eliminate every possibility**
- **reduces the likelihood of**

## Three-sentence synthesis

Write exactly three sentences:

1. describe how a possible exoplanet signal is first detected;
2. explain why scientists need to test alternative explanations;
3. explain what kind of additional evidence can make the interpretation more convincing.

If your answer clearly preserves **signal → alternatives → stronger evidence**, you have captured the article’s most transferable reasoning structure.
