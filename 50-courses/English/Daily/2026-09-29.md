## Why this is specifically worth using

Today deliberately rotates away from the recent sequence of earthquake systems, digital memory, linguistics, exoplanets, and consumer-interface design.

This National Archives piece is valuable because it trains a habit that matters far beyond history:

**a source is evidence, not a transparent window onto reality.**

A diary may be firsthand but selective. A photograph may look direct but still reflect framing, timing, and what was not captured. A government report may contain systematic data but also institutional assumptions. A textbook may synthesize many sources yet still depend on choices about what to include and how to interpret it.

That gives you a very reusable reasoning chain:

**source → context → point of view → corroboration → interpretation → tentative conclusion**

This chain is useful in academic reading, journalism, research papers, lab reports, policy analysis, and even ordinary online fact-checking.

## Important caveat — “primary” does not mean “automatically true”

The page was **last reviewed on August 15, 2016**, and it is written as guidance for educators rather than as a modern research review.

Its central pedagogical point remains useful: primary sources expose students to the fact that historical evidence must be interpreted.

But do not convert that into either of these bad conclusions:

1. **“Primary sources are more truthful than secondary sources.”**  
   Not necessarily. A firsthand witness can be mistaken, biased, incomplete, or strategically misleading.

2. **“Everything is subjective, so every interpretation is equally valid.”**  
   Also false. Interpretations can be compared by asking how well they account for the available evidence, whether they fit the source's context, and whether independent sources corroborate them.

A better formulation is:

> Historical knowledge is interpretive, but interpretations are still constrained by evidence.

That is the core precision target for today.

## Before reading — rank four kinds of evidence

Imagine you are reconstructing what happened during a student protest twenty years ago.

You have:

- a participant's diary;
- a newspaper report published the next morning;
- a police incident log;
- a photograph taken during the event.

Before reading the article, rank them from **most reliable** to **least reliable**.

Then add this sentence:

> My ranking is provisional because reliability depends on ___.

The goal is to notice that “source type” alone is not enough. Reliability is often **claim-specific**.

A photograph may be excellent evidence for who stood in one location at one moment but weak evidence for what happened an hour earlier.

## Evidence ladder — from source to conclusion

Practice the difference between five levels:

### Level 1 — source fact

> A letter was written by a particular person at a particular time.

This is a fact about the artifact.

### Level 2 — content claim

> The writer says that local prices were rising rapidly.

This is a fact about what the source reports, not yet proof that the report is accurate.

### Level 3 — interpretation

> The letter suggests that rising prices were causing anxiety in that household.

Plausible, but interpretive.

### Level 4 — corroborated inference

> Several independent letters, price records, and newspaper reports point to broader concern about rising prices.

Stronger because multiple sources converge.

### Level 5 — broad generalization

> Most households in the region experienced severe anxiety because of inflation.

This requires much more evidence about representativeness, measurement, and alternative explanations.

When reading history—or science journalism—many errors happen because people jump from Level 2 directly to Level 5.

## Precision task — “bias” is not the same as “false”

Rewrite these claims more carefully.

1. The diary is biased, so it is useless.
2. The photograph shows what really happened.
3. The government report is official, so it is objective.
4. Two witnesses disagree, so we cannot know anything.
5. The historian has a point of view, so the conclusion is merely opinion.

Useful structures:

- **should not be taken at face value**
- **provides evidence for... but not for...**
- **is consistent with...**
- **does not by itself establish...**
- **can be checked against...**
- **the disagreement may reveal...**
- **a point of view does not eliminate evidential constraints**
