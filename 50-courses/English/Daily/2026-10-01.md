## Why this is specifically worth using

Today deliberately moves away from the recent sequence of earthquake-system constraints, historical-source criticism, and olfactory neuroscience.

This article starts with a familiar-looking painting and then changes the reader's interpretation step by step. A newly rediscovered drawing connects the work to Fragonard's broader group of “fantasy figures.” Technical studies then show that the painted head was substantially revised after an earlier version had already dried. The article can therefore be read as a compact case study in how interpretation changes when **new evidence alters the object we think we are looking at**.

For English learning, it trains four useful skills at once:

1. **visual description** — describing composition, pose, texture, color, and gesture without vague words such as “beautiful”;
2. **process language** — explaining how an artwork was made, revised, covered, retained, and re-examined;
3. **evidence language** — separating what imaging reveals from what historians infer;
4. **uncertainty language** — recognizing when a question remains open even after better technology produces more information.

The central reasoning pattern is:

**surface appearance → newly discovered clue → technical examination → revised reconstruction → remaining uncertainty**

That pattern is useful far beyond art history. You can use the same logic when discussing a changed research hypothesis, a software bug reconstructed from logs, a historical event reinterpreted after a new document appears, or a medical diagnosis revised after new imaging.

## Important caveat — technical imaging can reveal changes, but not intention directly

The article presents strong physical evidence that Fragonard changed the painting: the pose and head were revised, and traces of an earlier costume element remain beneath the final surface.

But the most interesting questions are not all directly answerable:

- Why did he return to the canvas?
- Was he refining an artistic concept?
- Did he change models?
- Why is the related sketch unnamed?
- Who exactly was the sitter?

Technical evidence can show that a revision happened and help reconstruct aspects of an earlier state. It cannot automatically tell us the artist's private intention.

Keep three levels separate:

1. **direct observation:** what is visible in the painting or technical image;
2. **reconstruction:** what earlier state is supported by the physical evidence;
3. **interpretation:** what the change may mean about the artist's purpose, sitter, or concept.

The article itself ends with unresolved questions. That is a strength, not a failure.

## Before reading — describe before you explain

Open the page and look at the main image for about 30 seconds, but do not read the text yet.

Write four sentences:

1. one sentence about **pose**;
2. one about **color**;
3. one about **brushwork or texture**;
4. one cautious sentence about **mood**.

Use this rule:

> Describe what you can see before you infer what the subject feels.

Bad:
> The girl is lonely.

Better:
> The sitter is shown in profile with her attention directed toward the book, which creates a private, self-contained effect.

The second version marks the difference between observation and interpretation.

## First pass — follow the article's changing explanation

Read the article once without stopping to inspect every interactive image.

After reading, close the page and reconstruct the story in **six moves**:

1. What is visually distinctive about Young Girl Reading?

## Second pass — separate verbs of observation from verbs of inference

Read again and build two columns.

### Column A — evidence / observation verbs

Look for or paraphrase language such as:

- reveal
- show
- retain
- detect
- expose
- identify
- measure
- compare

These verbs usually point toward material evidence.

### Column B — interpretation / inference verbs

Look for or paraphrase language such as:

- suggest
- imply
- indicate
- may have
- could have
- appear to
- be consistent with
- account for

These verbs signal that the writer is moving from evidence toward an explanation.

Then answer:

Your answer should identify the transition from **“the painting was changed”** to **“why Fragonard changed it.”**

## Evidence ladder — what can each method establish?

Complete this table after the second pass:

| Evidence | What it can establish relatively well | What it cannot establish by itself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| visible final painting | current pose, colors, surface effects | original pose or motive for revision |
| rediscovered sketch | a visual link to the fantasy-figure group | certainty about the sitter's identity |
| technical imaging | hidden or altered features beneath the surface | the artist's private intention |
| paint-layer analysis | sequence and timing clues about revisions | the full social context of the decision |
| comparison with related works | stylistic similarities and recurring motifs | proof that every similarity has the same cause |

### Precision target

Avoid this shortcut:

> New technology solved the mystery.

A more precise conclusion is:

> New technical evidence changed the reconstruction of the painting's history while leaving some questions unresolved.

## Mini language focus — visual description without empty adjectives

Replace each vague sentence with something more specific.

1. The painting is **beautiful**.
2. The brushwork is **interesting**.
3. The colors are **nice**.
4. The sitter looks **mysterious**.
5. The composition is **good**.

Possible dimensions to specify:

- loose / controlled
- broad / narrow
- opaque / translucent
- warm / cool
- saturated / muted
- frontal / profile
- static / energetic
- layered / flat
- sharply defined / softly modeled

Example:

> Broad, visible strokes give the yellow fabric an energetic surface, while the face is modeled more carefully.

Do not copy a sentence from the source. Build your own description from what you observe.

## Output task — explain how new evidence can revise an interpretation

Give a **90-second spoken answer** to this prompt:

**When should new evidence make us revise an explanation rather than simply add another detail to it?**

Use this structure:

1. State the original interpretation.
2. Introduce the new evidence.
3. Explain what part of the old interpretation must change.
4. Identify what remains uncertain.
5. End with one sentence about why uncertainty should be preserved rather than “filled in.”

Try to use at least five items from today's vocabulary note:

**cast something in a new light, undergo revision, underlying, retain, trace, reconstruct, deliberate, alter, account for**

## Three comprehension / output questions

1. **Technique:** How does the article contrast Fragonard's rapid, energetic handling of paint with the more controlled treatment of the sitter's face?
2. **Evidence:** Why did the rediscovered sheet of sketches and the later technical studies matter together rather than separately?
3. **Judgment:** What can we say with relatively high confidence about the painting's revision, and what would still be speculation about Fragonard's motive?

For Question 3, use this frame:

> The evidence strongly supports ___. It is reasonable to consider ___, but the available evidence does not establish ___.
