## Why this is specifically worth using

This article is useful because it is not simply “a story about endangered languages.” It asks what it means for a language to remain usable in modern life.

The central problem is practical. A language can survive in speech yet still be difficult to use in digital settings if keyboards do not support the right characters, online reference tools are scarce, schools lack materials, or translation and text-to-speech systems do not work well for that language.

That gives you a strong reasoning chain:

**language survives in a community → digital infrastructure may still be missing → practical tools lower barriers → wider use becomes possible → long-term vitality still depends on people and institutions**

The article is also good for learning how feature writing mixes several evidence types:

- demographic background;
- historical context;
- quotations from educators and organizers;
- descriptions of concrete tools;
- statements about future plans;
- broader claims about identity, access, and language rights.

Your task is to keep those evidence types separate rather than treating every sentence as equally established.

## Important caveat — “digitization” is not the same as “preservation”

The title uses strong language: the initiative is described as “groundbreaking” and as bringing Mayan languages into the digital age.

That description is understandable, but it should not be treated as an independent impact evaluation.

The article profiles the Mayan Languages Preservation and Digitization Project and relies heavily on people involved in or connected with the project. It gives concrete examples of keyboards, glossaries, workshops, school use, and plans for speech and translation tools. Those examples show that practical digital infrastructure is being built.

They do **not**, by themselves, prove that a language will remain vigorous for generations.

Long-term language vitality can also depend on:

- whether children continue learning and using the language;
- whether schools and public institutions support it;
- whether speakers can use it in work, health care, law, media, and everyday communication;
- whether digital tools remain maintained and widely adopted;
- whether speakers themselves want and control those tools.

So keep this distinction:

**digital availability can support language use; it does not automatically guarantee long-term language vitality.**

## Precision drill — archive, access, use, vitality

These four ideas are related but not interchangeable.

### 1. Archive

To preserve records, recordings, texts, or descriptions for future reference.

### 2. Access

To make materials, services, or tools reachable and usable.

### 3. Use

To communicate, learn, create, work, or participate through the language.

### 4. Vitality

To remain actively transmitted and used across generations and social settings.

A language may be well documented in an archive but weakly used in daily life.

A digital keyboard may improve access without guaranteeing widespread uptake.

A language can also remain socially vital even when digital infrastructure is incomplete.

Rewrite each sentence so that it becomes more defensible.

2. Once a translation model exists, language barriers disappear.
3. Archiving a language is the same as keeping it alive.

5. Community-led projects are always more effective than university-led projects.

Useful structures:

- **can make it easier to...**
- **may help reduce...**
- **is one component of...**
- **does not by itself guarantee...**
- **its effectiveness depends partly on...**
- **the article suggests rather than demonstrates that...**

## Output task — explain the difference between a tool and an outcome

Give a **90-second spoken response**:

**Why can a useful digital tool matter without guaranteeing the long-term outcome its creators hope for?**

Use this structure:

1. Name one tool.
2. Explain the immediate barrier it reduces.
3. Explain what behavior would have to change for the tool to matter.
4. Name one institutional or social factor outside the tool itself.
5. State one kind of evidence that would show real impact over time.
6. Finish with a cautious conclusion.

Try to use at least five items from today's vocabulary:

**grassroots, be designed for, bridge a gap, on one's own terms, gain access to, pass down, be confined to, underrepresented, sustain**

## Three comprehension / output questions

1. **Mechanism:** Why can something as small as a keyboard layout matter for whether people use a language online?
2. **Governance:** Why does the article repeatedly emphasize that communities should help decide which tools are built and how they are used?
3. **Evaluation:** What evidence would you want before concluding that a digitization project has improved the long-term vitality of a language?

For Question 3, try:

> **The existing evidence shows ___. To establish a stronger long-term claim, I would also want data on ___.**
