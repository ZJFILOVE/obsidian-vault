# Daily English — Maps as Purpose-Dependent Representations

## Authentic input
**National Geographic Society — Map**  
Source: https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/map/  
Type: educational article; estimated reading time **8–10 minutes**; level CET-6 → introductory TOEFL.

**Why this source:** Maps offer a concrete way to reason about the difference between a useful model and a complete reproduction of reality. The text introduces spatial representation, symbols, scale, and projection; the most transferable skill is to identify *which properties a representation preserves and which it distorts*. The page is an educational overview, not a technical comparison of every projection; avoid claiming that a single projection is universally best.

## Pass 1 — Read for the argument (10 minutes)
Read without translating sentence by sentence. Mark the passage that explains each of these: the purpose of maps, what symbols communicate, how scale changes the amount of visible detail, and why representing a curved surface on a flat plane creates trade-offs. Close the page and write four sentences: (1) what a map is, (2) why abstraction is necessary, (3) one source of distortion, and (4) how intended use affects design.

## Pass 2 — Read for precision (12 minutes)
Reread and underline verbs indicating relationships rather than certainty: *represent, depict, indicate, preserve, distort, approximate*. For each, record the object and scope. Make a two-column comparison: a map intended for navigation versus one intended to compare areas. Which features matter most for each? Identify one example where a visually persuasive display could mislead without being factually fabricated.

## Output and shadowing (6–8 minutes)
Explain to an imaginary exchange student, in **60–90 seconds**, why a world map cannot preserve every spatial property. Use *depending on, at the expense of, in contrast, be suited to*. Then read aloud one short explanatory paragraph from the linked article (do not copy it into your notes), paying attention to sentence stress and contrastive phrases.

## Three comprehension/output questions
1. Why is simplifying geographical information necessary rather than automatically a defect?
2. What kinds of distortion can arise when representing Earth on a flat surface, and why does intended use matter?
3. Choose an everyday model (campus map, transit diagram, weather forecast). Explain in 80–100 English words one thing it captures well and one thing it leaves out.

## Precision drill
Rewrite each statement so its scope is defensible: **All maps are inaccurate**; **A larger map always contains more useful information**; **A projection that preserves direction must preserve area**. The target is a precise conditional statement, not simply adding *maybe*.
