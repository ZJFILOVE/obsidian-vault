**Making the Most of Your Mistakes**

### Why this one

This episode is a good bridge from textbook English to real academic conversation. The topic is also unusually useful for study and research: mistakes are inevitable when you are learning or trying something genuinely new, but not every failure is equally informative. Amy Edmondson distinguishes productive experimentation from preventable failure and discusses how people and organizations can learn from errors rather than merely hide them.

The speech is much more natural than a prepared TED talk: you will hear interviewer prompts, reformulation, examples, hedging, and academic ideas explained in accessible spoken English. The official page provides a transcript, so you can move between listening and text without relying on machine subtitles.

## Three-pass study plan

### Pass 1 — Gist, no transcript

Listen continuously. Do **not** pause for unknown words. Your only task is to reconstruct the argument.

After listening, answer from memory:

1. What distinction does Edmondson make between useful and avoidable failures?
2. Why can trying to eliminate every failure make learning harder?
3. What makes an experiment informative even when the desired result is not achieved?

Write a 4–6 sentence English summary before opening the transcript.

### Pass 2 — Detail + transcript

Listen again with the official transcript available. Pause only when a passage contains either an important argument or an expression you would actually reuse.

Focus on how speakers express:

- uncertainty rather than absolute certainty;
- cause and consequence;
- distinctions between similar concepts;
- lessons extracted from an unsuccessful outcome;
- organizational or research environments where people can report mistakes.

Do not collect every unfamiliar word. Add only expressions that you could plausibly use in a lab meeting, essay, presentation, or discussion.

### Pass 3 — Output + shadowing

Choose two stretches of roughly 45–90 seconds where the speaker explains an idea rather than tells a long story.

For each stretch:

1. listen once;
2. read the transcript and mark thought groups `/`;
3. shadow at the speaker's speed twice;
4. close the transcript;
5. explain the same idea in your own English for 60 seconds.

The goal is **not** to memorize the wording. Preserve the argument while changing the language.

## Chinese → English translation

Translate before opening the reference answer.

> 在科研中，失败本身并不值得赞美。真正重要的是，我们能否区分那些本可以通过遵守已有规范避免的错误，与为了探索未知问题而不得不承担的风险。如果一个实验建立在合理的假设之上，设计足够严谨，而且即使结果与预期不同仍然提供了新的信息，那么这种失败可能具有很高的学习价值。相反，如果团队成员因为害怕受到责备而隐瞒异常结果，小问题就可能不断累积，最终造成更大的损失。因此，一个好的研究环境不仅要鼓励创新，也要让人能够及时指出问题、讨论错误，并根据新证据调整方向。

> [!answer]- Reference translation
> In research, failure is not something to celebrate in itself. What matters is whether we can distinguish mistakes that could have been avoided by following established procedures from risks that must be taken when exploring genuinely unknown questions. If an experiment is based on a reasonable hypothesis, is designed rigorously, and still produces new information even when the result differs from what was expected, the failure may have substantial learning value. By contrast, if team members hide anomalous results because they are afraid of being blamed, small problems can accumulate and eventually lead to much greater losses. A good research environment should therefore not only encourage innovation, but also make it possible for people to speak up early, discuss mistakes, and course-correct in response to new evidence.

### Translation notes

- **failure is not something to celebrate in itself** avoids the unnatural idea that all failure is inherently good.
- **distinguish A from B** is a high-value academic structure.
- **established procedures** is more precise than simply saying “rules” in a research context.
- **anomalous results** is useful scientific English for results that deviate from expectation.
- **in response to new evidence** is a reusable academic chunk for evidence-driven revision.
