### Why this is worth using

This is a strong step away from the science-heavy material of the last several daily sessions. Instead of listening for factual explanation, you have to follow **claims, objections, distinctions, counterexamples, and revisions of a position**. That makes it unusually useful for academic speaking and argumentative writing.

Sandel's English is clear and deliberately paced, but the student responses make the session feel much more authentic than a scripted educational video. You will hear people hesitate, qualify a claim, defend an intuition, change direction, and respond to an objection in real time.

The source title is dramatic, but the lecture is not simply “about murder.” The first half uses deliberately simplified moral dilemmas to expose the assumptions behind consequentialist and categorical reasoning. The second half turns to a historical survival case and begins introducing utilitarianism. Treat the scenarios as tools for analyzing arguments, not as realistic decision guides.

## Useful words and chunks

### 1. moral dilemma
**Meaning:** a situation in which important moral considerations conflict  
**Collocations:** face a moral dilemma; present a dilemma; resolve a dilemma  
**Example:** The policy creates a moral dilemma because protecting one group may impose serious costs on another.

### 2. weigh the consequences
**Meaning:** carefully consider likely outcomes before deciding  
**Collocations:** weigh the consequences of a decision; weigh costs against benefits  
**Example:** A policymaker should weigh the consequences rather than focus on a single attractive outcome.

### 3. draw a distinction between A and B
**Meaning:** make an important conceptual difference explicit  
**Collocations:** draw a clear/sharp distinction; fail to distinguish A from B  
**Example:** We need to draw a distinction between causing harm intentionally and accepting a foreseeable side effect.

### 4. at stake
**Meaning:** involved in a situation and capable of being lost, harmed, or affected  
**Collocations:** lives are at stake; what is at stake; high-stakes decision  
**Example:** When public trust is at stake, a technically correct decision may still require careful explanation.

### 5. justify / justification
**Meaning:** show that an action, belief, or decision has adequate reasons  
**Collocations:** justify a decision; moral justification; be justified in doing something  
**Example:** A good intention alone may not be enough to justify a risky action.

### 6. objection
**Meaning:** a reason for disagreeing with an argument or proposal  
**Collocations:** raise an objection; respond to an objection; a serious objection  
**Example:** One objection to the proposal is that it treats unequal cases as if they were identical.

### 7. be consistent with
**Meaning:** fit logically with a principle, claim, or earlier position  
**Collocations:** logically consistent; remain consistent with; consistency of an argument  
**Example:** Your conclusion should be consistent with the principle you used earlier.

### 8. hypothetical scenario
**Meaning:** an imagined case used to test reasoning  
**Collocations:** consider a hypothetical; construct a hypothetical scenario  
**Example:** A hypothetical scenario can reveal hidden assumptions even when it is unrealistic.

### 9. on the face of it
**Meaning:** judging only from the first or most obvious appearance  
**Collocations:** on the face of it, this seems reasonable  
**Example:** On the face of it, the rule looks fair, but unusual cases expose its limitations.

### 10. principle
**Meaning:** a general rule or standard used to guide judgment  
**Collocations:** apply a principle; general principle; underlying principle  
**Example:** The difficult part is deciding whether the principle should apply to every similar case.

### 11. counterexample
**Meaning:** a case showing that a general claim may be false or too broad  
**Collocations:** provide a counterexample; test a claim against counterexamples  
**Example:** One counterexample can force us to revise an overgeneralized rule.

### 12. qualify a claim
**Meaning:** make a statement more limited or precise instead of presenting it as absolute  
**Collocations:** qualify an argument; heavily qualified conclusion  
**Example:** Strong academic writing often qualifies a claim rather than pretending that there are no exceptions.

## Chinese → English translation

Translate first; open the answer only afterward.

**在公共决策中，只比较“总收益”往往是不够的。一个方案即使能让大多数人受益，也可能把成本集中施加在少数人身上。因此，严谨的讨论不仅要估计政策可能产生的结果，还要说明哪些限制不能仅仅因为结果看起来有利就被忽视。另一方面，如果我们坚持某项原则，就需要检验自己是否愿意在相似情形中一致地应用它。好的论证并不是迅速宣布一个结论，而是在面对反例、异议和新的事实时，仍然能够解释为什么原来的原则应该保留、修改或放弃。**

> [!answer]- Reference translation
> In public decision-making, comparing aggregate benefits alone is often insufficient. A policy may benefit the majority while concentrating its costs on a relatively small group. A rigorous discussion should therefore not only estimate the likely consequences of a policy, but also explain which limits should not be ignored merely because the overall outcome appears favorable. At the same time, if we appeal to a principle, we should test whether we are willing to apply it consistently in similar cases. A strong argument does not simply announce a conclusion quickly; it remains able to explain, in the face of counterexamples, objections, and new facts, why the original principle should be retained, revised, or abandoned.

### Translation notes

- **aggregate benefits** is more natural in analytical writing than a literal *total benefits* when discussing society-wide outcomes.
- **concentrate costs on a group** is a useful policy/economics collocation.
- **merely because...** expresses “不能仅仅因为……就……” compactly.
- **appeal to a principle** means invoke a principle as a reason, not emotionally “appeal to” someone.
- **apply it consistently in similar cases** is a reusable argumentative structure.
- **retain, revise, or abandon** gives a clear three-way academic contrast.
