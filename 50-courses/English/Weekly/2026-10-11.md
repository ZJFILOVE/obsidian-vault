## Why this episode is worth listening to

This week deliberately rotates from last Sunday's science interview to an **unscripted sports-and-leadership conversation**. Megan Rapinoe (soccer), Sue Bird (basketball), and host Adam Grant discuss the difficulty of making younger teammates feel heard, what star players and uncelebrated supporting players contribute, and how elite athletes rebuild their identity after retirement.

The learning goal is to hear **claim → example → challenge → qualification → revised view**. Pay attention to changes in speakers, informal self-corrections and comparisons. In academic English, learn to separate anecdotal recollections from an explanatory framework or demonstrated research finding.

**Important caveat:** The title promises insights about effective teams, but an interview is not controlled evidence that a particular leadership practice works universally. The guests sometimes disagree or reframe their positions. The publisher warns that small differences may exist between the transcript and released recording. Advertising can affect runtime.

## Three-pass study plan (approximately 75–95 minutes)

### Pass 1 — Gist, no transcript (42–48 minutes)

Listen straight through, without pausing to translate. Divide your paper into three columns: **Adam / Megan / Sue**. Under each speaker, write two ideas they support and one moment of uncertainty. Then close the player and summarize the discussion in 80–100 words using the sequence **voice and authority → overlooked contributions → transition and identity**. Distinguish a speaker's first interpretation from their later reflection.

### Pass 2 — Detail and evidence (20–30 minutes)

Replay three 3–5-minute segments, using topic transitions rather than invented timestamps:

1. **Younger players and voice:** Why can a senior leader feel welcoming while other people hesitate to disagree?
2. **Soccer versus basketball:** Listen for less-visible contributions, team size, star-player effects, and disagreement about which sport relies more heavily on team culture.
3. **Life after retirement:** Listen for the disappearance of a familiar performance target, the challenge of being a beginner, and differences in feedback.

For each, create four columns: **speaker | claim | type of support | limitation**. Only after an initial replay consult the official TED transcript. Identify one example, one counterexample, and one claim that should be qualified before being generalized.

### Pass 3 — Speaking and shadowing (12–17 minutes)

Find a 30–45-second two-speaker exchange from the opening section. Shadow each voice on separate replays, focusing on stress at disagreement or qualification rather than copying filler words. Then deliver a two-minute response: **Why might a well-intentioned leader still discourage honest feedback?** Include a mechanism, an original example from outside sport, and a caveat. On your second attempt switch from informal to academic register.

## Twelve reusable words and chunks

All examples below are newly authored for this lesson, not copied from the podcast. Learn the phrase and its collocation, then substitute your own subject.

1. **have a say in** — 对……有发言权；*have a say in decisions*. Example: Junior researchers should have a say in how the laboratory is organized. Contrast: being allowed to speak does not guarantee being heard.
2. **speak up about** — 主动提出顾虑；*speak up about a problem*. Example: Staff should feel able to speak up about a safety concern. Contrast: *speak loudly* is about volume, not courage.
3. **earn someone's trust** — 赢得某人的信任；*earn respect; establish credibility*. Example: Managers earn trust when they follow through on promises. Contrast: a title alone does not establish trust.
4. **give credit to / take credit for** — 把功劳归给别人 / 归给自己；*give credit to the team*. Example: The article gives credit to the people who collected the data. Recognition should be specific, not inflated.
5. **carry one's weight** — 尽到自己那份责任（口语）；*everyone carries their weight*. Example: Fairness does not mean identical jobs, but everyone must carry their weight. Formal alternative: *contribute fairly*.
6. **make room for** — 为不同意见、活动或人留出空间；*make room for disagreement*. Example: The chair made room for objections before the final vote. It can mean social rather than physical space.
7. **be on the same page** — 对核心问题达成共识；*be on the same page about the goals*. Example: Before collecting data, we should be on the same page about the criteria. Agreement on aims is not unanimity.
8. **adapt to** — 适应；*adapt to change; adaptability; an adaptive response*. Example: New students need time to adapt to the demands of university courses. Use **adapt to**, not *adapt with*.
9. **move on from / let go of** — 走出旧阶段 / 放下；*move on from a role; let go of expectations*. Example: She moved on from a familiar job to learn another profession. Moving on need not imply failure.
10. **actionable feedback** — 可付诸改进的反馈；*constructive feedback; give specific feedback*. Example: The trainee requested actionable feedback after her presentation. **Feedback** is usually uncountable.
11. **in hindsight / look back on** — 事后看来 / 回顾；*in hindsight, it was...*. Example: In hindsight, the instructions were not clear enough. Watch out for hindsight bias.
12. **distinguish A from B** — 区分；*distinguish intentions from outcomes*. Example: We must distinguish an ambitious plan from one that has worked in practice. Also acceptable: *distinguish between A and B*.

**Retrieval:** Translate without looking: “新人应有发言权”；“领导需要赢得信任”；“事后看来，我们把笼统的表扬当成了有效反馈”。

## Five comprehension questions

Answer each in 3–5 English sentences and indicate whether your evidence is a guest's experience, a proposed explanation, or a research finding.

1. What does Rapinoe reconsider about her approach to younger players? Why can a sincere invitation to speak up still fail?
2. What different arguments do the guests offer about team culture in soccer versus basketball? What counterexample might challenge either argument?
3. Why can exaggerated praise of a supporting player feel less respectful than specific recognition of modest contributions?
4. How does retirement change the way elite athletes evaluate progress? Name one difficulty encountered in a new occupation.
5. Find an organizational distinction introduced by Grant. What does it illuminate, and why does it not prove a universal law?

> [!answer]- Evidence checkpoints
> 1. Status differences may discourage people even when a leader believes discussion is open.
> 2. Soccer includes less-visible supporting work; basketball's smaller groups create close interdependence. Neither comparison is universally established by the interview.
> 3. Accurate, specific recognition is more credible than implausible flattery.
> 4. Former athletes may miss immediate scores and actionable performance feedback when trying unfamiliar work.
> 5. A conceptual framework helps explain examples, but stronger claims require examining research design and context.
